Explore Asheville Sept. 2024 to Mar. 2025 Co-lead, PR department

Telling the truth about a city after Hurricane Helene

The challenge

Helene hit Asheville on September 27, 2024, and visitation stopped overnight in a region where tourism supports one in seven jobs. Our PR director and coordinator positions were both vacant, so a fellow PR manager and I co-led the department for six months. For the first weeks I worked from Charlotte, because Asheville had no reliable power, water or internet.

What I did

  • Helped set up a shared media tracker and coordinated 68 requests in five weeks, including CNN, NBC News, TODAY, The New York Times and the BBC. Most asked for our CEO, so I scheduled and prepared her for each interview.
  • Became an on-camera spokesperson for the first time, in dozens of interviews with outlets including WRAL, WCNC Charlotte, Spectrum News 1 and The Charlotte Observer.
  • Argued inside the marketing team that we couldn't wish our way out of the storm. Most of the visitor experience was intact, but the public believed Asheville was a disaster zone, so we named that perception and corrected it with current data, talking points and photos.

How the message changed, week by week

  1. Oct. 1Still standing, with a long road ahead. Focus on residents, first responders and donations.
  2. Week twoHow to help from afar: online shopping with local makers, the relief fund and Concert for Carolina.
  3. Week of Oct. 21Reopening in stages and pockets, naming what was open and what needed time.
  4. Early NovemberPlan your return trip with heart. You coming back is our comeback.
  5. Mid-NovemberBe Part of the Comeback, built around resilience and the holiday season.

Result: Months after the storm, Asheville made The New York Times' "52 Places to Go in 2025." In the quarter we led PR without a director, the team earned 265 placements. At the six-month mark I helped plan Good Morning America's live "Asheville Rising" broadcast, which drew more than 2.6 million viewers that week. GMA and its sponsors raised $2.7 million for Western North Carolina.

Explore Asheville 2024 to 2025 Co-lead, media campaign

Putting Black Asheville in the national story

The challenge

Asheville's new Black Cultural Heritage Trail marks a mile of history through three neighborhoods, including places that urban renewal tore apart. Destination marketing usually smooths over that kind of history. The campaign needed to tell it straight and send visitors to the Black-owned businesses that carry it forward.

What I did

  • Co-led the year-long media campaign. I designed the press trip and wrote the national press release, which spoke frankly about urban renewal and racial injustice.
  • Built the itinerary around more than 20 BIPOC-owned businesses and hosted 12 journalists.
  • Created a BIPOC press sheet on The Block, Asheville's historic Black business district, its Black Wall Street history and Cherokee influence on the region.
  • When a New York Times writer was undecided about including Asheville in "52 Places to Go in 2025," I pitched her new Black Asheville storylines. That gave the city a story beyond hurricane recovery.

Result: 30 placements, including Travel + Leisure, Bloomberg and The Washington Post, and a spot on The New York Times' "52 Places to Go in 2025."

RISE ABOVE, a newsletter by and for people living with HIV

The challenge

A grant from AIDS United funded a newsletter written by WNCAP clients for other clients. Several contributors had high needs, including health and transportation barriers.

What I did

  • Led a team of six current and former clients: led editorial meetings, made assignments and kept contributors on deadline.
  • Removed what kept people from showing up. I drove members to meetings and brought food, and one writer drove two hours from rural Clay County to take part.
  • Edited each issue and contributed to grant reporting.

Result: At least six seasonal editions, with a print run of about 1,000, reaching roughly 200 clients through community centers, public housing and coffee shops across Western North Carolina.

About

Why mission work

I grew up in the Northern Virginia suburbs and started my career in Washington, D.C., at the Americans for the Arts Action Fund. There I helped train grassroots advocates to question presidential candidates on camera about arts funding, and got on-the-record answers from Hillary Clinton, Bernie Sanders and several Republican candidates.

At WNCAP I led delegations of clients to lobby lawmakers in Washington and Raleigh on PrEP, Ryan White funding and harm reduction. My most important job was helping them tell their own stories.

At Explore Asheville I learned to work at national scale, and Hurricane Helene taught me that I do my best work when the stakes are high and the cause is real. Now I'm returning to mission-driven work full time: lifting up marginalized communities and defending democracy at a moment that calls for it.

Before communications, I trained in theater. It still shows up in how I write, pitch and appear on camera. I've been car-free for four years and will happily talk your ear off about transit and walkable cities.
